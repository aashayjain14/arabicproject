Arabic letters, growing on a palm tree

A school project by Aashay Jain. Twenty-eight letters, how each one sounds, how to write it, and the phrases you need to start a conversation.

Special Mention at Innovista 2024

Aashay took this project to Innovista 2024 and received a Special Mention award for it.

What is on the model

The project turns the Arabic alphabet into something you can look at, touch and scan.

The palm tree
A palm carved from painted foam. The letters run from the base of the model up into the fronds, each one on its own decagon coin in a different colour.
The QR codes
Every coin has a QR code beside it, so the model can be scanned as well as looked at.
The map
The palm grows out of a map of the UAE painted in the red, green, white and black of the national flag.
The desert scene
Below the tree is a small desert world: an Arabian oryx, a falcon on a watchtower, camels, a well, an oasis ringed with palms, and prints of calligraphy and a desert sunset.

From the workbench

Learn Arabic

Tap a coin to hear a letter and see how to write it. Arabic is read from right to left, so the alphabet starts at the top right.

This device has no Arabic voice installed, so the audio may be silent or read in another language. Adding an Arabic voice in your device's speech or language settings fixes it.

    A little about Arabic

    Right to left

    Arabic is written and read from right to left. There are no capital letters.

    Letters change shape

    Most letters have up to four forms, depending on whether they stand alone or sit at the start, middle or end of a word.

    Vowels are tiny marks

    Small marks above and below the letters show the short vowels. Everyday writing usually leaves them out.

    One of six UN languages

    Arabic is an official language of the United Nations and is spoken by hundreds of millions of people.

    Many spoken versions

    The formal written language is shared across the Arab world, while everyday speech changes from region to region, including Gulf Arabic.

    Words you already use

    English borrowed these from Arabic.
